# v3.18.0 — a release stops being an outage, and the auditor learns to count

**Starting the fleet no longer starts it all in one second.** Seventeen minutes
after jawata-mcp v4.0.0 published, studio restarted all three residents onto the
new engine at the same instant and each began a cold workspace import: 189%, 211%
and 133% CPU plus children, roughly ten of twenty cores, load average 17, and a
machine its owner described as stuck for minutes.

`start_all_runtimes` spawned them back to back with nothing in between. It now
waits between *distinct workspaces* — ten seconds by default, settable by an
environment variable. Projects sharing a workspace collapse into one spawn, and
projects that merely join a running process cost nothing to wait for, so the
delay is paid once per real spawn.

The total work is unchanged; only its concentration moves. That is the point —
the peak is what a person feels, and three cold imports beginning in the same
second is a peak nobody asked for. It also scaled the wrong way: the more
workspaces you keep, the worse the simultaneous start.

This is half of the repair. The other half shipped as jawata-mcp v4.0.1, which
makes each re-import cheap by remembering resolved classpaths across a restart.
Neither alone is enough: staggering three expensive imports still burns the cores
in sequence, and three cheap ones starting together still spike.

**The sprint auditor gained a check that would have caught a sprint's own name.**
Sprint 28d was named for Fowler's refactorings. Its spec said, in its own
summary, that jawata performs about 15 of Fowler's ~60 and that the sprint builds
the missing ones. Its first deliverable then scoped the survey to the *other*
half of the sprint — the pattern catalogue's shapes — and returned 8 candidates,
of which 4 shipped. Coverage moved from ~15 to ~18 of 60. Every checkpoint
passed, because the survey's measure was met exactly as written, over a
population the requirement never named.

The new check: **a quantity must reach a deliverable that produces it.** Extract
the number verbatim, carry it into the traceability row, state each deliverable's
output size, compare arithmetically. A selecting deliverable owes a *floor* —
unstated is unbounded, and unbounded is the finding, because a ranking is a
priority order and not a commitment. A selection step's input sets its output, so
an input drawn from another part of the sprint makes that part the scope-setter.
And a quantity in the title or task sentence must be traced to a deliverable that
produces it even where nothing selects.

The traceability matrix gains the two columns the check needs: the requirement's
quantity, and the output size of the deliverable answering it. Without them the
check has nowhere to land.
